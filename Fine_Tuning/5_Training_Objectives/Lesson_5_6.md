# Lesson 5.6 — SLM Training Strategies: How Small Language Models Are Built Differently

## The Small Model Problem

A 7B parameter model trained the same way as a 70B model — same data, same objectives, same architecture just smaller — will always be worse. That is expected. What is less expected, and more interesting, is how much of that gap can be closed by training differently.

The Phi series from Microsoft demonstrated this. Phi-1 (1.3B parameters) outperformed GPT-3 (175B parameters) on Python coding benchmarks. Phi-2 (2.7B) matched or exceeded models 5–10× its size on many academic benchmarks. The models were not better because of architecture — they were better because of data strategy.

The core insight: **at small parameter counts, data quality and training strategy matter more than scale.** Every parameter must work harder. You cannot afford to waste capacity on noise.

---

## The Four Pillars of SLM Training

### Pillar 1: Data Quality Over Scale

Large models can learn from noisy data because they have enough capacity to separate signal from noise. Small models cannot. A 2B model trained on 300B tokens of noisy web text is worse than the same 2B model trained on 30B tokens of carefully curated, high-quality text.

**What "high quality" means for SLM training:**
- **Educational density:** Does this text teach something? Does it contain reasoning, explanation, or structured knowledge? Random forum posts do not. Textbooks, tutorials, and well-explained Stack Overflow answers do.
- **Correctness:** Factually wrong text must be filtered. For small models, wrong facts are more likely to stick because there is less capacity to model uncertainty.
- **Linguistic quality:** Well-structured prose teaches better language patterns than fragmented, informal text.

**The Phi approach ("textbooks are all you need"):**
The Phi-1 paper showed that 7B tokens of "textbook quality" coding examples — synthetically generated to be clear, educational, and complete — outperformed models trained on hundreds of billions of raw code tokens. Quality created a ~30× apparent efficiency advantage.

### Pillar 2: Synthetic Data at Scale

If high-quality real data is scarce, generate it. The entire Phi series relies heavily on synthetic data generated by GPT-4:

- **Synthetic textbooks:** "Write a chapter explaining recursion in Python with worked examples and exercises."
- **Synthetic exercises:** "Generate 50 Python programming exercises of increasing difficulty with solutions."
- **Synthetic reasoning traces:** "Walk through this math problem step by step."

The generated data has specific properties: it is pedagogically structured, it contains explicit reasoning, it covers exactly the concepts you want, and you can generate exactly as much as you need.

**The risk:** synthetic data created by GPT-4 inherits GPT-4's biases, blind spots, and error patterns. Diversity of generation prompts, human review of samples, and evaluation against held-out real benchmarks are essential quality controls.

### Pillar 3: Knowledge Distillation

Distillation trains a small "student" model to learn from the outputs of a larger "teacher" model. Instead of — or in addition to — training on one-hot ground truth labels, the student learns from the teacher's full probability distribution over the vocabulary.

**Why teacher distributions are more informative:**

For the next token after "The capital of France is ___":
- Ground truth (hard label): `Paris` with probability 1.0
- Teacher distribution (soft label): `Paris: 0.97, Versailles: 0.01, Lyon: 0.008, ...`

The soft distribution tells the student that Versailles is somewhat related to France and capitals (even if wrong), that Lyon is vaguely plausible, etc. This is compressed knowledge about the model's world understanding — a much richer training signal than just "the answer is Paris."

**The KL divergence loss:**

```python
# Standard cross-entropy loss (hard labels)
loss_hard = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, target_token_ids)

# KL divergence loss (soft labels from teacher)
teacher_probs = F.softmax(teacher_logits / temperature, dim=-1)
student_log_probs = F.log_softmax(student_logits / temperature, dim=-1)
loss_soft = F.kl_div(student_log_probs, teacher_probs, reduction="batchmean")

# Combined loss
loss = alpha * loss_hard + (1 - alpha) * loss_soft * (temperature ** 2)
```

The `temperature` parameter (> 1) "softens" the distribution — spreading probability mass to non-peak tokens. Higher temperature = more knowledge transfer about the full distribution. The `temperature²` scaling corrects for the gradient magnitude change introduced by temperature.

**Types of distillation:**

| Type | What is transferred | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Response distillation | Teacher's final outputs/responses | Simplest; train student on teacher's generated text |
| Logit distillation | Teacher's full probability distributions | More information-dense; requires running teacher at training time |
| Feature distillation | Intermediate hidden state representations | Matches internal representations, not just outputs |
| Attention distillation | Teacher's attention maps | Teaches similar attention patterns |

For practical SLM training, **response distillation is most common** — collect outputs from GPT-4 or Claude, fine-tune the small model on them. Logit distillation is more effective but requires simultaneous access to the teacher model during training (compute-intensive).

### Pillar 4: Capability Focus

A 2B model cannot be equally good at everything. Trying to make it general-purpose spreads its capacity too thin. Instead, identify 2–3 key capabilities the model must excel at and optimize training data, objectives, and evaluation around those.

**Example: Phi-3-mini**
- Primary capabilities: reasoning, math, code
- Training data weighted heavily toward educational content in these domains
- Benchmark targets: MMLU (knowledge), HumanEval (code), GSM8K (math)
- The model deliberately sacrifices performance on creative writing, roleplay, and open-ended dialogue to maximize the target capabilities

This is a product decision masquerading as a training decision. Know what your SLM will be used for and optimize ruthlessly for that.

## Architecture Choices in SLMs

SLMs are not just smaller LLMs — they often have architectural modifications that improve inference efficiency:

**Grouped Query Attention (GQA):** Reduces KV cache size (Lesson 11.2). Essential for SLMs deployed on edge devices with limited memory.

**Shared input/output embeddings:** Use the same embedding matrix for token input and output prediction. Saves ~(vocabulary_size × d_model) parameters — significant at small model sizes.

**Sliding Window Attention (SWA):** Mistral and derivatives use SWA, limiting attention to a fixed window of recent tokens. Reduces memory and compute at inference, enabling longer effective context on limited hardware.

**Smaller FFN ratio:** Standard LLMs use FFN intermediate dimension = 4× model dimension. Some SLMs use 2.67× (SwiGLU gate-up-down structure) to reduce parameter count in FFN without proportionally reducing model dimension.

## Case Study: How Phi-3-mini (3.8B) Was Built

Microsoft's Phi-3-mini achieves performance competitive with much larger models through deliberate data strategy:

1. **Filtered web data:** Web text filtered through educational quality classifiers — pages that explain, teach, or demonstrate something score higher; random blogs and social media score low.

2. **Synthetic textbooks:** GPT-4 generates structured "textbook chapters" covering coding, math, reasoning, and general knowledge topics. Each chapter has explanations, examples, and exercises.

3. **Synthetic conversations:** GPT-4 generates realistic dialogue examples covering instruction following, reasoning questions, and coding tasks.

4. **Architecture:** 3.8B parameters, 32 layers, GQA (32 query heads, 4 KV heads), context length 128K (via LongRoPE).

5. **Training:** Standard LM pre-training on the curated corpus, followed by SFT and safety alignment.

Result: on MMLU, Phi-3-mini scores 68.8 — higher than LLaMA-3 8B's 66.6, despite having less than half the parameters. On math (GSM8K), it scores 82.5 vs 79.6 for LLaMA-3 8B.

## When SLM Training Strategies Apply to Larger Models Too

The SLM principles are not exclusive to small models. They are extreme expressions of universal training truths:
- Data quality matters at all scales — just more so for small models
- Synthetic data is a tool for any model — small models just depend on it more
- Distillation helps even 7B and 13B models learn better from 70B+ teachers
- Capability focus benefits any fine-tuning effort — know what you are optimizing for

The practical upshot: if you are fine-tuning a 7B model for a specific task, apply SLM thinking. Curate the training data ruthlessly. Generate synthetic examples for gaps. Distill from a larger model where possible. Define 2–3 core capability targets and measure against them rigorously.

---

## Summary

- SLMs (< 7B parameters) cannot learn efficiently from noisy data — their limited capacity requires high-quality, educational-density training data.
- **Data quality over scale:** Phi-1 demonstrated that 7B tokens of "textbook quality" synthetic data outperforms hundreds of billions of noisy web tokens for coding capability.
- **Synthetic data:** GPT-4-generated textbooks, exercises, and reasoning traces fill gaps where real high-quality data is scarce. Risk: inherits teacher model biases; mitigate with human review and benchmark evaluation.
- **Knowledge distillation:** Student learns from teacher's probability distributions (soft labels), not just ground truth tokens. Soft labels carry compressed knowledge about related concepts and the model's uncertainty. Temperature controls how soft the distributions are.
- **Capability focus:** Small models cannot be good at everything. Identify 2–3 target capabilities, weight training data toward them, and accept reduced performance elsewhere.
- Architecture choices (GQA, shared embeddings, sliding window attention) are integral to SLMs — they are designed for inference efficiency on edge hardware, not just parameter reduction.
