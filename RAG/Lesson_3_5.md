# Lesson 3.5 — Hypothetical Document Embeddings (HyDE) and Query-Side Augmentation

---

## The Query-Document Asymmetry Problem

Standard dense retrieval embeds both queries and documents using the same model and measures cosine similarity between their vectors. The assumption is that semantically related queries and documents will land near each other in the embedding space.

This assumption holds well when the query and document are written in similar styles and at similar length. It breaks down when they are not.

Consider the typical RAG scenario:
- **Query:** "maternity leave duration" — 3 words, a noun phrase, no context, written in search engine style.
- **Document chunk:** "Employees who have worked for the company for at least 12 months are entitled to up to 16 weeks of fully paid maternity leave, commencing no earlier than 4 weeks before the expected birth date..." — 40+ words, full sentences, formal document style, rich with context.

These two texts live in very different regions of the embedding space even though they are semantically related. The query is a sparse signal; the document chunk is a dense, contextually rich one. The embedding model trained on (query, document) pairs learns to bridge this gap, but it does so imperfectly — especially for short queries, domain-specific queries, or queries that do not resemble the training distribution.

HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings, Gao et al., 2022) attacks this asymmetry directly. Instead of embedding the short query and hoping it lands near the relevant document, generate a hypothetical document that looks like what a relevant document chunk would say, and embed that instead.

The hypothesis lives in "document space" rather than "query space." The asymmetry disappears.

---

## How HyDE Works

The process is straightforward:

```
Standard retrieval:
  Query → embed → query_vector → ANN search → relevant chunks

HyDE retrieval:
  Query → LLM → hypothetical answer → embed → hyp_vector → ANN search → relevant chunks
```

**Step 1:** Take the user's query.

**Step 2:** Ask an LLM to generate a hypothetical document — a passage that could plausibly be the answer to the query. The LLM generates this from its parametric knowledge, not from the actual corpus. It may be factually wrong. That does not matter.

**Step 3:** Embed the hypothetical document (not the query).

**Step 4:** Use the hypothetical document's embedding as the query vector for ANN search.

The insight: a hypothetical answer, even if factually incorrect, has the same vocabulary, sentence structure, and topical density as real document chunks that answer the question. Its embedding will be close to real relevant chunks in the embedding space.

---

## A Concrete Example

**Query:** "what are the tax implications of early 401k withdrawal"

**Hypothetical answer generated by LLM:**
"Early withdrawal from a 401(k) retirement account before age 59½ generally results in two tax consequences. First, the withdrawn amount is added to your ordinary income and taxed at your marginal federal income tax rate. Second, the IRS imposes a 10% early withdrawal penalty on the distribution amount. Certain exceptions exist that waive the 10% penalty, including permanent disability, substantially equal periodic payments (SEPP or 72(t) distributions), separation from service at age 55 or older, medical expenses exceeding 7.5% of adjusted gross income, and qualified domestic relations orders (QDRO). State income taxes may also apply depending on your state of residence."

This hypothetical answer:
- Uses vocabulary that appears in financial planning documents ("marginal federal income tax rate", "early withdrawal penalty", "SEPP", "72(t) distributions", "QDRO").
- Has the sentence structure and length of a real document chunk.
- Even though some details may be wrong or outdated, the embedding of this text will land near actual financial planning document chunks that discuss the same topic.

The embedding of the raw query "what are the tax implications of early 401k withdrawal" does not have any of this domain vocabulary or document-style structure. It is a weaker retrieval signal by comparison.

## Implementation

```python
async def generate_hypothetical_document(
    query: str,
    domain_context: str = "",
    document_style: str = "informational article",
    llm_client = None
) -> str:
    """
    Generate a hypothetical document passage that could answer the query.
    The passage should resemble actual documents in the corpus.
    """
    
    system_prompt = f"""You are writing content for a {document_style}.
Your task is to write a passage that directly answers the given question.

Important:
- Write as if this is an excerpt from an actual {document_style}
- Use formal, technical language appropriate to the domain
- Write 100-200 words
- You may not know the exact correct answer — write a plausible answer
- Do NOT say "I don't know" or hedge — write confidently as a document would
- Do NOT include the question in your response
- Start directly with the answer content"""

    if domain_context:
        system_prompt += f"\nDomain context: {domain_context}"
    
    response = await llm_client.chat.completions.create(
        model="gpt-4o-mini",
        messages=[
            {"role": "system", "content": system_prompt},
            {"role": "user", "content": f"Question: {query}\n\nPassage:"}
        ],
        max_tokens=300,
        temperature=0.3  # Slight randomness is fine — plausibility over accuracy
    )
    
    return response.choices[0].message.content.strip()


async def hyde_retrieve(
    query: str,
    vector_db,
    embedding_model,
    llm_client,
    k: int = 20,
    use_both: bool = True  # Whether to also retrieve with original query
) -> list[dict]:
    """
    HyDE retrieval: embed hypothetical document instead of (or alongside) query.
    
    use_both=True: retrieve with both HyDE and original query, merge with RRF
    use_both=False: retrieve with HyDE only (higher risk, higher potential reward)
    """
    
    # Generate hypothetical document
    hyp_doc = await generate_hypothetical_document(query, llm_client=llm_client)
    
    # Embed the hypothetical document
    hyp_embedding = await embedding_model.embed(hyp_doc)
    
    # Retrieve using hypothetical document embedding
    hyde_results = await vector_db.search(
        collection="documents",
        query_vector=hyp_embedding,
        limit=k
    )
    
    if not use_both:
        return hyde_results
    
    # Also retrieve with original query embedding (safety net)
    query_embedding = await embedding_model.embed(query)
    query_results = await vector_db.search(
        collection="documents",
        query_vector=query_embedding,
        limit=k
    )
    
    # Merge both result sets with RRF
    return reciprocal_rank_fusion([hyde_results, query_results])
```

## HyDE Variants

### Single Hypothetical Document

The basic version: generate one hypothetical answer, embed it, retrieve. Simple and often effective.

**Risk:** The LLM generates a hypothetical answer that is confidently wrong or off-topic. The embedding then points to wrong chunks.

### Multiple Hypothetical Documents

Generate multiple hypothetical answers and average their embeddings. This produces a centroid in the embedding space that is more robust than any single hypothetical.

```python
async def hyde_multi_retrieve(
    query: str,
    vector_db,
    embedding_model,
    llm_client,
    n_hypotheticals: int = 5,
    k: int = 20
) -> list[dict]:
    """
    Generate multiple hypothetical documents, average their embeddings.
    More robust than single HyDE — individual hallucinations are averaged out.
    """
    import numpy as np
    
    # Generate n hypothetical documents in parallel
    hypotheticals = await asyncio.gather(*[
        generate_hypothetical_document(query, llm_client=llm_client)
        for _ in range(n_hypotheticals)
    ])
    
    # Embed each hypothetical
    embeddings = await asyncio.gather(*[
        embedding_model.embed(hyp) for hyp in hypotheticals
    ])
    
    # Average the embeddings
    embedding_matrix = np.array(embeddings)
    mean_embedding = embedding_matrix.mean(axis=0)
    
    # Normalize the averaged embedding (cosine similarity requires unit vectors)
    norm = np.linalg.norm(mean_embedding)
    mean_embedding = (mean_embedding / norm).tolist()
    
    # Retrieve using averaged embedding
    results = await vector_db.search(
        collection="documents",
        query_vector=mean_embedding,
        limit=k
    )
    
    return results
```

Averaging embeddings is mathematically valid for cosine similarity search as long as you renormalize the result. The averaged embedding points toward the centroid of the hypothetical documents in the embedding space — usually closer to the true relevant cluster than any individual hypothetical.

**Cost:** N hypothetical generation calls + N embedding calls. For N=5, roughly 5× the cost of single HyDE. Typically worth it when retrieval quality is critical.

### HyDE with Document Style Matching

The quality of HyDE retrieval depends on how well the hypothetical document resembles actual corpus documents in style and vocabulary. If your corpus is legal contracts and you generate a hypothetical in plain English conversational style, the embedding will not land near the actual contract clauses.

Provide document style context in the generation prompt:

```python
DOMAIN_STYLES = {
    "hr_policies": "formal HR policy document with precise language about employee rights and procedures",
    "legal_contracts": "formal legal agreement with defined terms, whereas clauses, and numbered sections",
    "technical_docs": "technical documentation with step-by-step instructions and code examples",
    "financial_reports": "financial analysis document with specific figures, percentages, and fiscal period references",
    "medical_guidelines": "clinical practice guideline with evidence-based recommendations and medical terminology"
}

hyp_doc = await generate_hypothetical_document(
    query=query,
    domain_context="employee benefits and leave policies",
    document_style=DOMAIN_STYLES["hr_policies"],
    llm_client=llm_client
)
```

The closer the hypothetical matches your corpus style, the better the embedding alignment.

## When HyDE Works and When It Does Not

### HyDE Works Well When

**Short queries with little retrieval signal.** A 2-3 word query gives the embedding model almost nothing. HyDE converts this sparse signal into a rich, document-like passage.

**Queries using non-document vocabulary.** When the user writes conversationally ("how do I get out of my lease") and documents use formal language ("lease termination clause"), HyDE bridges the vocabulary gap because the hypothetical uses formal document language.

**Knowledge-rich domains where LLM has strong priors.** For common knowledge domains (general HR policies, standard financial regulations, common software engineering practices), the LLM's hypothetical is plausible and domain-appropriate.

**Open-domain Q&A where the topic is well-known.** Medical, legal, and financial questions over general reference material benefit significantly from HyDE.

### HyDE Fails When

**Proprietary or internal knowledge.** The LLM has no knowledge of your internal policies, specific product configurations, or company-specific processes. The hypothetical it generates will be generic and may not resemble your actual documents at all.

Example:
- Query: "what is the approval process for Project Athena budget changes"
- LLM hypothetical: generic text about project budget approval processes
- Your actual document: "All budget modifications to Project Athena above $50K require written approval from both the Finance Director and the VP of Engineering via the internal Budget Change Request (BCR) form 2024-BF"

The LLM has no way to know about Project Athena, BCR forms, or your specific approval thresholds. The generic hypothetical will not point toward your specific document.

**Very specific factual queries.** "What was the net income for fiscal year 2023 quarter 3?" — the LLM will generate a plausible number, but that number may be completely different from the actual figure. The embedding of a hypothetical with the wrong number may not retrieve the correct chunk.

**Technical queries with precise syntax.** "How do I configure the kubectl context for a multi-cluster setup?" — the LLM may generate plausible Kubernetes configuration, but if your internal docs use specific non-standard tooling or configurations, the hypothetical will diverge.

**Short, highly specific factual lookups.** "What is the employee referral bonus amount?" — the hypothetical adds noise without benefit. The original query is already precise enough.

### The Decision Rule

```python
def should_use_hyde(query: str, corpus_type: str) -> bool:
    """
    Heuristic to decide whether HyDE will help for this query.
    """
    query_words = query.split()
    
    # Too short — HyDE helps by expanding sparse signal
    if len(query_words) <= 3:
        return True
    
    # Internal/proprietary corpus — LLM has no relevant knowledge
    if corpus_type in ["internal_policies", "proprietary_docs", "product_specific"]:
        return False
    
    # Contains specific codes, numbers, or identifiers — HyDE may distort them
    import re
    if re.search(r'\b[A-Z]{2,}\d+\b|\b\d{4,}\b|\b[A-Z]{3,}\b', query):
        return False
    
    # General knowledge query on well-known domain
    if corpus_type in ["general_reference", "public_regulations", "standard_practices"]:
        return True
    
    # Default: use with original query as backup
    return True  # Always safe if combined with original query retrieval
```

## Combining HyDE with Your Existing Pipeline

HyDE is a query-side augmentation — it fits cleanly into the existing retrieval pipeline as an additional query vector source. You do not have to choose between HyDE and your existing retrieval. Use both and merge with RRF.

```python
async def full_retrieval_pipeline(
    query: str,
    conversation_history: list[dict],
    retriever,
    llm_client,
    embedding_model,
    corpus_type: str = "general"
) -> list[dict]:
    """
    Full retrieval pipeline: query understanding + HyDE + hybrid retrieval + merge.
    """
    all_result_lists = []
    
    # 1. Query understanding (rewrite/expand/decompose)
    processed_queries = await process_query(query, conversation_history, llm_client)
    
    # 2. Retrieve for each processed query (dense + sparse, merged internally)
    for q in processed_queries:
        results = await retriever.hybrid_retrieve(q)
        all_result_lists.append(results)
    
    # 3. HyDE retrieval (only if corpus and query type benefit from it)
    if should_use_hyde(query, corpus_type):
        hyde_results = await hyde_retrieve(
            query=query,
            vector_db=retriever.vector_db,
            embedding_model=embedding_model,
            llm_client=llm_client,
            use_both=False  # Original query already retrieved in step 2
        )
        all_result_lists.append(hyde_results)
    
    # 4. Merge all result lists with RRF
    merged = reciprocal_rank_fusion(all_result_lists)
    
    return merged[:50]  # Top 50 for re-ranking
```

HyDE adds one LLM call (hypothetical generation) and one embedding call. For most queries this is 150–300ms additional latency. Only worth it when the query is likely to benefit.

## HyDE vs. Query Expansion: Choosing Between Them

Both HyDE and query expansion try to improve retrieval by generating additional query signals. They are different tools for different problems.

| | HyDE | Query Expansion |
|---|---|---|
| **What it generates** | A hypothetical answer (document-style) | Alternative query formulations (query-style) |
| **Embedding** | Embeds the hypothetical answer | Embeds alternative queries |
| **Primary benefit** | Bridges query-document style gap | Increases vocabulary coverage |
| **Works for short queries** | Yes (primary use case) | Partially |
| **Works for proprietary corpus** | No | Yes |
| **Latency** | 1 LLM call + 1 embed | 1 LLM call + N embeds + N retrievals |
| **Risk** | Hallucinated hypothetical misdirects | Noisy variants dilute results |

**Use HyDE when:** Query-document style mismatch is the problem (short queries, conversational queries on formal documents, general knowledge domains).

**Use query expansion when:** Vocabulary coverage is the problem (multiple ways to say the same thing, domain synonym handling, parallel retrieval paths).

**Use both when:** You have the latency budget and the query could benefit from both — run them in parallel and merge all result lists with RRF.

## Evaluation: Measuring HyDE's Contribution

Do not assume HyDE is helping just because it sounds theoretically sound. Measure it.

```python
async def ab_test_hyde(
    test_queries: list[str],
    ground_truth: dict,  # query -> list of relevant chunk_ids
    retriever_without_hyde,
    retriever_with_hyde,
    k: int = 10
) -> dict:
    """Compare retrieval quality with and without HyDE."""
    
    results_without = []
    results_with = []
    
    for query in test_queries:
        # Without HyDE
        baseline_results = await retriever_without_hyde.retrieve(query)
        baseline_ids = [r["chunk_id"] for r in baseline_results[:k]]
        
        # With HyDE
        hyde_results = await retriever_with_hyde.retrieve(query)
        hyde_ids = [r["chunk_id"] for r in hyde_results[:k]]
        
        relevant = set(ground_truth.get(query, []))
        
        if relevant:
            baseline_recall = len(set(baseline_ids) & relevant) / len(relevant)
            hyde_recall = len(set(hyde_ids) & relevant) / len(relevant)
            
            results_without.append(baseline_recall)
            results_with.append(hyde_recall)
    
    import numpy as np
    return {
        "baseline_recall_at_k": np.mean(results_without),
        "hyde_recall_at_k": np.mean(results_with),
        "improvement": np.mean(results_with) - np.mean(results_without),
        "queries_where_hyde_helped": sum(
            1 for w, h in zip(results_without, results_with) if h > w
        ),
        "queries_where_hyde_hurt": sum(
            1 for w, h in zip(results_without, results_with) if h < w
        )
    }
```

This evaluation answers the critical question: not just "does HyDE improve average recall" but "how often does it help vs. hurt?" If HyDE hurts recall on 30% of queries while improving 70%, you need to understand the pattern of failures before deploying it globally.

---

## Summary

- Dense retrieval suffers from a query-document style asymmetry: short colloquial queries do not embed close to long formal document chunks despite semantic similarity.
- HyDE bridges this gap by generating a hypothetical document-style answer using an LLM and embedding that instead of the raw query. The hypothetical lives in "document space."
- Factual accuracy of the hypothetical does not matter — only style and vocabulary alignment matter for embedding quality.
- Multiple hypothetical documents with averaged embeddings are more robust than a single hypothetical, at N× the generation cost.
- HyDE works best for: short queries, conversational queries on formal documents, general knowledge domains where the LLM has strong domain knowledge.
- HyDE fails for: proprietary internal corpus (LLM has no knowledge of it), specific factual lookups, queries with precise codes or identifiers.
- Combine HyDE with original query retrieval using RRF — never rely on HyDE alone for safety.
- Always measure HyDE's contribution on your specific corpus. Theoretical benefit does not guarantee empirical benefit on your data.
